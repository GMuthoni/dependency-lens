Important distinction

A dependency is not automatically a bottleneck. It becomes a meaningful bottleneck only if failure, scarcity, maintenance burden, cost, or scaling difficulty at that dependency materially limits the outcome.

The idea

When technologies look different, ask what they still have in common.

When technically different approaches are compared, the obvious differences tend to dominate the analysis.

Different architectures. Different interfaces. Different business models. Different technical trade-offs.

But technologies ultimately have to accomplish something in the real world.

What condition must all of these approaches make work, regardless of how they achieve it?

Sometimes the important risk is not visible at the level where the technologies differ. It sits one level above them — in a shared dependency.

Dependency Lens is a small analytical instrument for surfacing and testing those dependencies.

The method

Surface → Function → Dependency → Exposure → Test

Surface What makes them look different?
Function What must each accomplish?
Dependency What must remain true?
Exposure Why does convergence matter?
Test What would falsify it?

01 · Surface

Start with what makes the approaches appear different. What architecture, mechanism, interface, or implementation choice separates them?

02 · Function

Move one level up. What does each approach actually have to accomplish for the real-world outcome to occur?

This is where apparently different technologies can begin to converge.

03 · Dependency

Ask what condition must remain true for that function to work.

Candidate dependencies might include maintaining usable performance as conditions change, handling exceptions without excessive human intervention, integrating into an existing workflow, maintaining calibration or state, sustaining throughput under variable inputs, or keeping operating and maintenance burden within acceptable limits.

These are candidate dependencies, not conclusions.

04 · Exposure

If several approaches depend on the same condition, ask whether that creates meaningful shared exposure.

A common dependency does not automatically mean a common bottleneck. It becomes important when failure, scarcity, cost, maintenance, operational burden, or scaling difficulty at that dependency materially limits the outcome.

05 · Test

Finally, make the hypothesis vulnerable.

What observable evidence would support it — and, more importantly, what evidence would make us abandon it?

A useful dependency should lead to a measurable prediction or a meaningful falsifier.

Why compare this way?

Differentiation can hide concentration.

Company-by-company and technology-by-technology analysis is often organized around differentiation.

That makes sense when the question is: Which approach is technically better?

It is less useful when the question is: What could still prevent several technically different approaches from succeeding?

Two architectures can solve a problem differently while still being exposed to the same underlying condition.

That creates a form of concentration that may be missed in a conventional comparison.

This does not replace feature analysis.

Dependency Lens adds another analytical layer rather than asking analysts to ignore technical differences.

The discipline is knowing when not to force convergence.

Sometimes technically different approaches genuinely depend on different conditions. The method should be allowed to conclude that.

A simple example

Warehouse automation

Consider three approaches to warehouse automation: autonomous mobile robots, fixed robotic picking systems, and human-operated picking supported by software.

Their architectures are obviously different.

A conventional comparison might focus on throughput, capital cost, flexibility, accuracy, and deployment requirements.

What must all three approaches do when the real operation encounters something the system did not expect?

That leads to a candidate dependency: exception handling and recovery.

The interesting question then becomes measurable: how many human interventions are required per 1,000 tasks, and how long does recovery take?

The hypothesis could be wrong. If one architecture demonstrates high throughput with very low intervention requirements across realistic operating conditions, the shared-dependency thesis becomes much weaker.

That is the point.

What this is — and isn't

A hypothesis generator, not a truth machine.

Dependency Lens is

A comparison instrument; a way to move from technological differences to shared operating conditions; a hypothesis- generation and testing aid; and a way to compare multiple approaches to the same consequential problem.

It is not

A generic AI framework, company-ranking system, replacement for technical diligence, or claim that every technology hides the same bottleneck.

Intellectual position

The proposition is deliberately narrower than the method's vocabulary.

Functional decomposition, dependency analysis, constraint analysis, systems engineering, technology assessment, bottleneck analysis, and cross-case comparison all have substantial precedents.

The narrower proposition is this:

A deliberately cross-architecture comparison, organized around shared function and followed by an explicit falsification step, can expose an important dependency that is easy to miss when each technology is analyzed primarily inside its own vocabulary.

Whether that proposition is useful is an empirical question.

That is what the tests are for.

Status

An experimental analytical instrument.

The current implementation is deliberately small.

The objective is not to build the largest framework.

It is to see whether one sharper question can improve how technically different approaches are compared:

What do these technologies have in common that their differences make easy to overlook?

And then:

What evidence would prove us wrong?
