Dependency Lens · v0.3

The bottleneck may be one level above the technology.

Compare apparently different approaches by what they have to accomplish, what they depend on, and what evidence would prove or disprove a shared dependency.

For high-consequence systems, the useful comparison is rarely “which architecture is best?” It is often “what must remain true across all of them—and where would failure of that condition become consequential?”
Instrument

1. Define the problem

Start with the real-world job shared across the approaches. Describe the consequence you care about rather than choosing from a preset outcome list.

2. Add the approaches

These can be technologies, architectures, vendors, clinical approaches, products, processes, or competing solutions. Describe the meaningful technical difference—not why one is better.

3. Choose what you want to discover

Cross-domain stress tests

Try to break the Lens.

These examples test whether the same analytical operation remains useful when technologies, risks, and operating environments change radically.

01

BCI

Pass · preliminary
Does the Lens reveal a dependency that conventional architecture-level comparison misses?
Finding

Long-term state maintenance

Endovascular, high-channel-count cortical, and alternative cortical interfaces differ substantially in how they access neural signals. Yet each ultimately has to maintain a usable relationship between neural activity, decoded intent, and system action.

Candidate dependency:
Maintaining a usable neural-to-action control loop as biological, behavioral, decoder, and device conditions change.
Challenge

Recalibration or adaptation alone does not establish a bottleneck. The decisive question is whether the intervention required to preserve useful control becomes a recurring operational constraint.

Conclusion

Different BCI architectures may share a dependency that is invisible at the surface-architecture level. The hypothesis becomes consequential only if longitudinal evidence shows that maintaining useful control materially limits independence, cost, performance, or scalability.

Surface differences

The architectures solve neural access differently. The Lens asks what must remain true after deployment.

  • Endovascular neural access
  • High-density cortical recording
  • Alternative cortical interface architectures
  • Signal acquisition and decoding
  • Longitudinal usability
Useful measures
Specialist interventions per user; recalibration frequency; performance degradation between interventions; restoration time; independent-use rate.
02

Warehouse automation

Pass · strong preliminary
When radically different automation architectures encounter reality outside the nominal workflow, what must they recover from?
Finding

Exception handling

Mobile robots, fixed robotic picking systems, and human picking with software assistance place automation in different parts of the warehouse workflow. But all must maintain order-processing flow when inventory, routes, objects, equipment, or human activity depart from the expected state.

Candidate dependency:
Recovering from deviations without requiring human intervention at a rate that materially limits throughput, reliability, or scalability.
Challenge

Exception handling is not automatically a bottleneck. A high exception count may be operationally irrelevant if recovery is fast, autonomous, and cheap.

Conclusion

The Lens changes the comparison from nominal automation performance to the cost of leaving the nominal state. The candidate becomes a bottleneck only when intervention frequency, recovery time, or resulting downtime materially constrains useful warehouse output.

Surface differences

Automation can be mobile, fixed, integrated, or centered on human execution with software assistance.

  • Autonomous mobile robots
  • Fixed / integrated robotic picking
  • Human picking with software assistance
  • Fleet and workflow coordination
  • Human intervention and recovery
Useful measures
Human interventions per 1,000 tasks; mean exception-resolution time; autonomous-resolution rate; downtime; manual takeover rate; intervention burden as scale increases.
03

Water treatment

Pass · preliminary
Can physically different treatment mechanisms be compared through the condition required to keep producing acceptable water when the input changes?
Finding

Maintaining useful throughput under changing inputs

Membranes, thermal processes, and adsorption systems use fundamentally different physical mechanisms. Yet each must translate variable feed water into treated water that remains within the required specification at useful throughput.

Candidate dependency:
Maintaining acceptable water quality and useful output as feed conditions vary, without an unsustainable increase in energy, material consumption, or maintenance.
Challenge

Fouling, scaling, saturation, and energy demand are technology-specific mechanisms. The Lens should not erase those differences; it should test whether their operational consequences become comparable at a higher level.

Conclusion

The useful comparison is not simply which mechanism removes a contaminant best under favorable conditions, but how much useful output deteriorates—or operating burden rises—when the input becomes less favorable.

Surface differences

The mechanisms differ radically. The shared question concerns sustained treatment performance.

  • Membrane filtration
  • Thermal treatment
  • Adsorption-based treatment
  • Changing feed composition
  • Intervention and resource burden
Useful measures
Treated-water quality; useful volume per unit time; throughput decline; energy per acceptable output; material consumption; intervention frequency; recovery time.
Evidence standard

Keep the inference visible.

The site separates what the literature establishes from what the Lens infers and from what remains unresolved.

Claim

What an external source establishes about the technology, operating environment, performance, or known constraint.

Inference

What the Lens proposes by comparing different approaches at the level of shared function and dependency.

Uncertainty

What has not yet been demonstrated—including whether the candidate dependency actually becomes a bottleneck.

The proposition

Don't ask what all technologies have in common.

Ask at what level they become meaningfully comparable—and then make the comparison vulnerable to evidence that could prove it wrong.

Shared dependency → Does it actually constrain anything?
